Core Guide

The 3-Phase Business Dining Guide

Step-by-step guidance designed specifically for university students preparing for their first formal business meals and client dinners.

Phase I

Pre-Meal Planning & Preparation

Planning ahead, picking the right restaurant, and arriving on time.

  • Be Punctual: Arrive 10–15 minutes early if you are hosting to check the table; arrive exactly on time if you are a guest. Arriving late can ruin a good business impression.
  • Pick the Right Restaurant: Choose a quiet place where you can talk easily and where service is reliable. Use apps like OpenTable to review the menu and reserve a quiet table beforehand.
  • Dress Professionally: Casual does not mean careless. Dress appropriately for the restaurant (for example, a clean blazer, collared shirt, and dress pants).
  • Check Food Restrictions: Ask guests about food allergies or dietary preferences (vegetarian, halal, gluten-free) 24 hours before the meal.
Host Tip: Never discuss prices in front of guests. Call the restaurant manager or host in advance to keep a credit card on file for payment.
Source: Chaney & Martin (2007) & Business Management Daily
Phase II

At the Table

Place settings, utensils, and basic table manners.

  • The "BMW" Table Rule: Bread plate on the Left, Meal plate in the Middle, Water & Wine glasses on the Right.
  • Napkin Rules: Put it on your lap right after you sit down (or after your host does). If you leave the table for a moment, place it on your chair. When finished, place it loosely to the left of your plate.
  • Outside-In Rule: Use your utensils from the outside in as each course is served.
  • Choose Easy-to-Eat Food: Avoid messy foods (chicken wings, long spaghetti, whole crab). Choose dishes you can eat cleanly with a fork and knife.
  • Follow Your Host: Wait for your host to take their first bite before you start eating.
Bread Rule: Never cut bread with a knife. Break off one small bite with your fingers, butter that piece, and eat it.
Source: Schweitzer (2018) & Carreño (1853)
Phase III

Paying the Bill & Following Up

Paying the bill, leaving a proper tip, and building the business relationship.

  • The Host Pays: The person who invited the other to dinner pays the whole bill. Never argue over the check or ask to split it item-by-item.
  • Leave a Good Tip (20% Standard): Tipping well reflects on your company. A 20% tip is standard in business dining in the US.
  • Send a Thank-You Note Within 24 Hours: As Keith Ferrazzi writes in Never Eat Alone, send a personalized email or thank-you note within 24 hours. Mention something specific from your conversation.
  • Save Your Meeting Notes: Write down meeting notes, next steps, and client details in your CRM or notebook right after the meal.
Follow-Up Tip: Say thank you, mention an important point from the conversation, and suggest the next step.
Source: Keith Ferrazzi (2005) & Indeed Career Guide (2026)

Practical Menu Guide

Business Menu Guide & Ordering Tips

Choosing what to order at a business meal is important. Use the filters below to find safe main dishes, avoid messy foods, and master wine and drink etiquette.

Recommended Main Dish Easy to Eat
Grilled Salmon Fillet

Salmon is tender and easy to eat with a fork. It does not splatter sauce and has no bones to worry about.

Dining Tip: Great choice for easy conversation. Pairs well with white wine or chilled water.
Recommended Main Dish Easy to Eat
Filet Mignon (Tender Beef Steak)

Tender meat that is easy to cut into small bites. Order medium so there are no messy juices on the plate.

Dining Tip: Cut one piece at a time. It looks neat and is simple to eat while talking.
Recommended Main Dish Vegetarian Choice
Mushroom Risotto (Creamy Rice)

Eat quietly with a fork from the outside edges inward. Its creamy texture means no sauce splatters and no knife needed.

Dining Tip: A great vegetarian option that works well for almost any guest.
Recommended Main Dish Safe Choice
Boneless Herb Chicken Breast

Boneless chicken breast with light sauce. Easy to cut with a knife and fork without making a mess.

Dining Tip: A safe and reliable choice that lets you focus 100% on the conversation.
Messy Food (Avoid) High Risk
Spaghetti & Long Pasta with Red Sauce

Twirling long pasta can easily splash red sauce onto your shirt, jacket, or tie.

Dining Tip: Slurping pasta is bad manners. If you want pasta, choose bite-sized shapes like penne or ravioli.
Messy Food (Avoid) High Risk
Whole Lobster & Crab Legs

Cracking shells requires bibs, tools, and messy fingers, and juices can easily splash across the table.

Dining Tip: Never wear a bib at a business dinner. If you want seafood, choose peeled shrimp, fish, or scallops.
Messy Food (Avoid) Moderate Risk
French Onion Soup with Melted Cheese

Melted cheese sticks to the bowl and creates long, hot cheese strings that are difficult to cut while talking.

Dining Tip: Choose a smooth soup instead, and spoon it gently away from you across the bowl.
Messy Food (Avoid) High Risk
Barbecue Ribs & Chicken Wings

Eating with your hands leaves barbecue sauce on your fingers, making it impossible to shake hands or pass papers.

Dining Tip: Avoid finger foods during business dinners. Stick to foods you can eat cleanly with a fork and knife.
Drink Etiquette Core Rule
Let Your Guest Order Drinks First

Always let the client or guest order their drink first. If they order water or iced tea, do the same; do not drink alcohol alone.

Dining Tip: If the client orders wine, limit yourself to one glass, and drink water to stay sharp and clear-headed.
Glass Etiquette How to Hold
Holding Wine Glasses & Tasting

Hold all wine glasses by the thin stem, never by the bowl. Holding the bowl warms the drink and leaves fingerprints on the glass.

Dining Tip: When the server pours a small sample, take a gentle sip and nod if it tastes good. Do not drink it all in one gulp.
Drink Pairing Simple Guide
Simple Wine Pairing Rule

White wines (like Sauvignon Blanc) go best with fish, chicken, and salads. Red wines (like Cabernet) pair best with steaks and red meat.

Dining Tip: If you are unsure, simply ask the server: "Which wine pairs best with our guest's main dish?"

Dining Styles

Utensil Styles: American vs. Continental

In business dining, knowing both American and Continental styles shows good manners and confidence.

U.S. Standard

The American Method (Switching Hands)

Step 1: Cutting Food Hold your fork in your left hand (points down) and knife in your right hand. Cut one or two bite-sized pieces at a time.
Step 2: Placing the Knife Place the knife near the upper right edge of the plate, with the cutting blade facing inward toward you.
Step 3: Switching Hands Switch your fork to your right hand with the points facing up, and bring the food to your mouth.
Utensil Signals
  • Resting Position: Place your knife near the top edge of the plate and your fork on the lower right of the plate (points facing up).
  • Finished Signal: Place your knife and fork side by side diagonally across the plate, with fork points UP and knife blade facing inward.
  • Common in: Standard business dinners across the United States.
International Standard

The Continental Method (No Switching)

Step 1: Keep Hands Assigned Keep the fork permanently in your left hand (points facing DOWN). Keep the knife permanently in your right hand.
Step 2: Eat Without Switching Hands Cut one bite with your right hand. Bring the food directly to your mouth with your left hand, keeping fork points facing DOWN.
Step 3: Gentle Grip Rest your index fingers gently along the top of each utensil for control. Never hold utensils like pencils or clubs.
Utensil Signals
  • Resting Position: Cross your knife and fork in the center of the plate in an upside-down "V", with fork points facing DOWN.
  • Finished Signal: Place your knife and fork side by side diagonally across the plate, with fork points facing DOWN.
  • Common in: International business dining (Europe, Latin America, Asia); smooth and widely used worldwide.
Classic Etiquette Rules

Classic Etiquette Rules: Carreño's Code

Written by Manuel Antonio Carreño in 1853, the Manual of Civility and Good Manners is a classic guide on polite behavior, respect at the table, and treating others with kindness.

Classic Manners Book (1853)
Foundational Manners

"The dining table is one of the places where a person's degree of education and culture is most clearly and promptly revealed, for so numerous and diverse in nature are the rules that decency demands there..."

— Manuel Antonio Carreño | Chapter IV - VA: "At the Table", Section 1 (1853)
Pillar 1 • Respecting the Table

Keep the Table Clean and Clear

Carreño explains that the dining table is meant for enjoying food and good conversation.

  • No Personal Items: Never put phones, keys, hats, or folders on the dining table. Keep them in your pocket or bag.
  • Use the Napkin Properly: Use your napkin only to gently dab your lips before and after drinking. Never use it to wipe cutlery, sweat, or your nose.
  • Good Posture: Sit up straight and do not slouch. Keep your wrists near the edge of the table, and keep your elbows off the table while eating.
Pillar 2 • Respecting Waitstaff

How to Treat Servers and Waitstaff

Carreño points out that how you treat restaurant servers shows your true character and leadership.

  • Use Eye Contact: Call your server quietly with eye contact or a small hand gesture. Never shout, snap your fingers, or clap.
  • Speak Quietly: If there is a problem with your food or utensil, tell the server quietly and politely without making a scene.
  • Business Impact: Recruiters and clients notice how you treat servers. Being rude to staff shows poor social skills.
Pillar 3 • Respecting Your Companions

Conversation and Table Companions

A business meal is a shared experience. Guests and hosts should keep conversation positive and pleasant.

  • Topics to Avoid: Avoid arguments about politics, religion, illness, or personal problems that make guests uncomfortable.
  • Follow the Host: Wait until your host starts eating or drinking before you take your first bite. Respond politely to toasts.
  • Drink in Moderation: Do not drink too much alcohol or eat too fast. Professional behavior requires self-control.
Helpful Solutions

Handling Awkward Table Situations

How to stay calm and polite when small accidents, dropped utensils, or awkward moments happen during a meal.

Table Accident

Dropped Utensil on Floor

A fork or spoon accidentally slips and falls to the restaurant floor.

What to Do: Never crawl under the table to get it. Leave it on the floor. Calmly catch the server's eye and whisper: "Could I please have a clean fork when you have a moment?"
Social Situation

Food Stuck in Someone's Teeth

You notice parsley or food stuck in your client's or colleague's teeth during conversation.

What to Do: Never mention it in front of the group. If you are speaking one-on-one, touch your own teeth quietly as a gentle hint, or excuse yourself so they have a chance to check a mirror.
Table Spill

Spilled Water or Wine

A drink is accidentally knocked over and spills onto the tablecloth.

What to Do: Stay calm. Apologize simply: "Sorry about that, let me help." Dab with your cloth napkin to catch the liquid, and let the server change the cloth and glass.
Paying the Bill

Guest Wants to Pay the Bill

You invited the client, but they insist on taking out their card to pay.

What to Do: Smile and say: "It is our pleasure to invite you tonight; you can invite us next time!" Then pay the bill smoothly.
Drinks

Client Orders Alcohol, You Don't Drink

The client orders an alcoholic drink and asks if you would like one too.

What to Do: Order without hesitating or making excuses: "I will have sparkling water with lime, please." You do not need to explain why you are not drinking.
Phone Ringing

Urgent Phone Call Vibrates

Your phone vibrates in your pocket with an urgent message from work.

What to Do: Never put the phone on the table. Excuse yourself politely: "Please excuse me, I need to step away for a minute to answer an urgent work call." Place your napkin on your chair.

Business Dining Pocket Cheat-Sheet

Technical Sales & Marketing (SIE 415-515) • Andre Velazco
1. The BMW Table Rule
  • B = Bread Plate: on your LEFT. Break small bite-sized pieces with your fingers; do not cut rolls in half with a knife.
  • M = Meal: in the CENTER (your main plate).
  • W = Water & Wine: on your RIGHT. Hold wine glasses by the stem, not the bowl.
2. Carreño's Key Rules
  • Clean Table & Posture: Keep phones, keys, and papers off the table. Sit up straight and keep elbows off the table while eating.
  • Respect Servers: Call your server with eye contact or a small nod. Never snap your fingers or shout.
  • Follow the Host: Wait for your host to take the first bite or drink before you begin.
3. Utensil Signals
  • Outside-In Rule: Start with the utensils on the outside and work inward with each course.
  • Resting Signal: Place fork and knife in an upside-down "V" on your plate.
  • Finished Signal: Place knife and fork side by side diagonally across the plate.
4. Quick Solutions
  • Dropped Utensil: Leave it on the floor. Calmly ask the server for a clean one.
  • Accidental Spill: Dab with your cloth napkin to catch liquid; let the server change the cloth.
  • Paying the Bill: The person who invited pays. Give your card to the restaurant manager or host in advance.
